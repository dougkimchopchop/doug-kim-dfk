Essay Practice

How to get a piece you're proud of into the bank every week. You copy other writers privately to learn. You publish only your own material, poured into a shape you borrowed.

The rule Never ship work I'm not proud of. I can only keep that rule and still ship weekly if the piece is small. So the piece gets small, and the bar stays where it is.

The Week · feeds the bank

This fits the Writing Plan. Thursday publishes from the bank, never from this week's draft. This week's piece goes into the bank. The model you copy each week counts as your one craft study for that week.

Daily
20 minutes copying this week's model by hand. No analysis. You're learning its rhythm through your hand.
Mon
Draft in one sitting. Don't edit. Pour your own material into the model's shape.
Tue
Cut it by 30%. Start with the first paragraph and every sentence that explains.
Wed
Run the checklist. If it passes, it goes into the bank.
Thu
Publish the oldest piece in the bank. Pull one line and one frame from it as a Note.
The bar: the best you can make this piece this week. Not the best thing you'll ever write.
Three phases · 12 weeks
Weeks 1–4 · Dispatch length
One moment, one photograph
  • 300–600 words, one scene, one image, no thesis sentence
  • Models: Ross Gay, Teju Cole, Craig Mod, Doyle's "Joyas Voladoras"
  • Pick the frame first. Write only what it can't show: the smell, what was said, what happened right after
  • Change one thing a week (tense, person or length) so you know what worked
Weeks 5–8 · 800–1,500 words
Borrow a classic structure
  • "A Hanging" → Varanasi, done fully: you're a witness to a death, and you're implicated
  • "Stranger in the Village" → Romania: you're the outsider, and the village stares back
  • "Once More to the Lake" → a place you went back to, with a last line that turns the whole piece
  • "Joyas Voladoras" → one small thing that escalates into everything
Weeks 9–12 · Essay length
You at stake
  • "Shooting an Elephant": a moment you did something because people were watching
  • "Goodbye to All That": a city used to measure who you were
  • Pull from parked drafts: "Depression and Newtonian Physics," "Idle Time and Being Bored"
Drills · private, never published

This is the Writers Studio idea. Imitate the narrator's stance and the shape of the piece, never the words.

1
Copy by hand. 20 minutes a day, longhand. Watch where the sentences get short, where they get long, and where a paragraph breaks earlier than you'd break it.
2
Franklin's method. Jot a short note on each sentence of a passage. Three days later, rewrite the passage from your notes alone and compare. Each gap is a skill you don't have yet.
3
Map the moves. Label every paragraph with a verb (place / zoom / swerve / confess / undercut / land). Write your own piece to the same list. Start with Gay's "Loitering."
4
Stance swap. Orwell is ashamed and precise. Didion is cool and self-indicting. Bourdain is a gleeful insider. Rewrite Cairo from Bourdain's stance.
5
Caption essay. Teju Cole's method. Write 150 words that argue with one photograph or extend it. If a sentence repeats what the frame shows, cut it.
6
One-sentence scene. Write a whole scene as a single sentence, the way Kincaid does in "Girl" and Doyle does at the end of "Joyas Voladoras." It teaches momentum.
7
Adjective fast. Rewrite a paragraph with no adjectives, then put back only one. This kills the habit of stringing them together ("welcoming and wary, suspicious and angry").
8
Reverse-engineer a viral post. Take one from Substack B. Write down the headline promise, the hook, the structure, and the payoff. Then write your own version, privately. You're learning the packaging, not the content.
9
Ten last lines. Write ten, then keep the shortest one that still lands. Your best endings ("And she did and it does," "The revolution can wait") are all under eight words.

Wednesday checklist · before it goes in the bank
1
Read it aloud, all the way through.
2
Delete the first paragraph. If the piece starts better without it, keep it deleted.
3
Find every sentence that tells the reader what to feel. Replace it with an image or cut it.
4
Cut every pair or string of adjectives down to one.
5
Keep only the swears that hit. Keep two asides in parentheses at most.
6
Write three possible last lines. Pick the shortest one that lands.
7
Check typos, names, diacritics, and that terms stay consistent (Ganga or Ganges, never both).
8
Make the subtitle a concrete line pulled from the piece. Put the Subscribe button after the first payoff.
All yes? It goes in the bank. Next week's piece starts Monday.
