Storytelling Basics

The same mechanics run under a short story, a personal essay, a magazine feature, a documentary and a feature film. What changes is the tools you have and the promises you’ve made about truth.

The ten basics
1
Someone wants something. A person with a want gives the reader something to follow. In an essay that person is you, built on purpose as a character (Gornick’s persona).
2
Something’s in the way. No obstacle, no story. It can be a person, the world, or the character’s own flaw. The best ones are aimed at that flaw (Arndt).
3
Something’s at stake. What’s lost if this fails? Arndt looks for three layers: outside, inside, and what it would mean for how to live.
4
One thing causes the next. Trey Parker’s rule: between beats, you want “therefore” or “but.” If “and then” belongs there, you have a list of events.
5
It escalates. George Saunders: “a continual system of escalation.” Each beat should raise the stakes or shift them. Repeating a beat doesn’t count.
6
There’s a question hanging. Ira Glass calls it bait. Raising a question early promises an answer, and that’s what pulls the reader along.
7
You see it happen. Scenes hold what’s specific: a place, a body, a line someone actually said. Summary moves time. Use it, but know which one you’re in.
8
Somebody changes. Or fails to, or the reader’s understanding does. Something at the end has to be different from the start.
9
It means something. Glass’s second building block: the anecdote and the moment of reflection. A sequence of events with no reflection can feel pointless. Reflection with no events is a lecture.
10
The ending is surprising and inevitable. Arndt’s test: a good ending surprises while staying logical. A great one also says something about how to live. Andrew Stanton’s commandment holds throughout: make me care.
The story spine · test any idea in a minute
“Once upon a time there was ___. Every day, ___. One day ___. Because of that, ___. Because of that, ___. Until finally ___.”Rule #4, Pixar’s 22 Rules, compiled by Emma Coats

It works on nonfiction too. Here’s “Shooting an Elephant”:

Once
there was a British police officer in Burma who hated his job.
Every day
the locals jeered at him.
One day
an elephant killed a man, and he was sent to deal with it.
Because
the animal had calmed down, he decided not to shoot.
But
a huge crowd was watching, so he had to.
Finally
he admitted he’d done it to avoid looking a fool.

If you can’t fill this in for your own piece, the trouble isn’t the sentences.

Three shapes that work everywhere
Man in a hole
Trouble, then the climb out. One of Vonnegut’s story shapes. Most travel mishap stories are this. Ask what’s different when you’re out.
Mystery
A question, clues, then an answer that changes the question. Gladwell’s “Ketchup Conundrum.” Sarah Polley’s Stories We Tell digs into her own family this way.
The job
A plan, a team, things going wrong. Man on Wire tells a high-wire walk as a heist. It gives any true story an engine if someone is trying to pull something off.

For essay-specific shapes like the braid and the borrowed form, go to Structure.

What changes by form

Fiction

Special powerYou can enter any mind, and invent whatever the story needs.

The jobEarn belief. Keep the reader asking what happens next and why. Saunders reads stories line by line to find what makes you read the next one.

StudyChopin, “The Story of an Hour”, and Saunders’s book A Swim in a Pond in the Rain.

Personal essay & memoir

Special powerYou can think on the page. Lopate: the reader gets both the self who lived it and the self who understands it now.

The jobMake yourself a character, and find the story in the situation. No inventing.

StudyYour Structure page and Essay Practice.

Narrative nonfiction & reporting

Special powerYou can go find out. Sources, documents and experts can be turned into plot.

The jobPick a question and a person to carry it, and keep the complications in. Your access and your facts have to hold up.

StudyGladwell, “The Ketchup Conundrum” with Nieman’s reading, and Jack Hart’s Storycraft.

Documentary

Special powerReal faces and real time. The viewer sees for themselves.

The jobSheila Curran Bernard’s line: these stories depend “not on creative invention” but “on creative arrangement.” Choose your point of attack, where the film starts, and what the viewer knows when.

StudyMan on Wire, Stories We Tell, and this review of Bernard’s book.

Feature film

Special powerImage, sound, performance. Behavior can replace explanation entirely.

The jobEverything has to be visible or audible, and time is fixed. So structure carries more of the weight, and endings get built first. Pixar’s saying, via Arndt: the last thing you end up doing is the first act.

StudyArndt’s Endings and Beginnings, and Andrew Stanton’s TED talk.

Audio

Special powerA voice in someone’s ear. It’s intimate, and the listener can’t skim.

The jobKeep moving. Alternate anecdote and reflection, and keep raising questions. Your drafts can borrow this by being read aloud.

StudyIra Glass on the building blocks, plus Gladwell’s Revisionist History.

Ten questions for any story
1
Who’s it about, and what do they want?
2
What’s in the way?
3
What’s lost if it fails?
4
What question is the reader holding right now?
5
Is every link “but” or “therefore”?
6
Does it get harder as it goes?
7
Where do I actually see it happen?
8
What’s different at the end?
9
What did I come here to say?
10
Why should a stranger care?
