Furious Action Plan + Friction Maxxing Plan

You become a person of ferocious action by getting good at the moment you usually switch away. You’ve already named the thought that makes the switch feel reasonable. “I should get organized first.” That’s the moment to practice.

Use CBT at the decision point
  1. Catch the thought. Say it in the exact words it came in. “I need a clear head before I can write this.”
  2. Turn it into a prediction. “If I start with a scattered head, I won’t produce anything I can use.”
  3. Run the experiment. Open the piece and draft the next scene for 20 minutes. Start with one thing you actually saw. At the end, look at what you wrote. Did you get a usable sentence? Did the scene come into focus as you worked?
  4. Return the instant you notice you’ve switched. Close the tab. Put your hands back on the work. Each time you return while the urge to leave is still there, you practice the behavior you want to be able to summon under pressure.
Give the hard work a finish line

Pick one thing to finish today that would change your situation. Define it in a sentence with a verb you can verify.

  • Writing: “Publish the essay.” If you’re still drafting, “Finish the draft through the last paragraph.”
  • Money: “Open the IRS notices, write down the tax years and balances, and make the call.”
  • Photography: “Make the edit, sequence the photographs, and export the set.”

Then do the part you’ve been working around. If you keep revising the opening, write the ending. If you keep organizing the tax folder, open the notice you’re avoiding. If you keep tweaking the edit, make the final cut.

Your cliff jump today is the first consequential action you’ve been postponing. Put the exact task in front of you, give it 20 minutes, and decide what comes next with evidence from the work itself. At the end of the day, write two lines. What did I finish? What thought did I act through?

I can feel the resistance and still move. Do the hard thing. Finish it.

Friction maxxing plan

Build an appetite for demanding work. Practice anticipating the satisfaction of engaging, enjoying the effort as it happens, and wanting to return. Give “addicted to friction” a concrete meaning through chosen, repeatable actions.

  1. Rehearse wanting it. Before starting, remember a demanding experience you enjoyed. Camera in hand, completely absorbed. A difficult sentence finally taking shape. Feel what you liked about being that engaged. Say, “I want that feeling again. This task gives me somewhere to find it.”
  2. Make friction your cue to approach. When you feel boredom, uncertainty, or reluctance, practice answering, “Here’s my opportunity to lean in.” Open the difficult document. Write the awkward paragraph. Begin the conversation. Pair that cue with an immediate action.
  3. Find pleasure inside the struggle. As you work, notice each satisfying detail. Your attention settling. A confusing problem becoming understandable. A decision made. A sentence improving. Pause briefly to recognize it. “This feels good. I’m enjoying working through it.” Use words that match what you actually feel.
  4. Reward staying while you are staying. When the urge to escape appears and you complete the next step, acknowledge that moment. “I wanted to leave, and I stayed. That was satisfying.” Give yourself credit during the effort so finishing has rewards along the way.
  5. Make tomorrow’s reward vivid. Picture waking up with the essay published, the application submitted, or the listing live. Name what completion gives you. “I want the freedom of having this finished. I’m willing to work for it now.”
  6. Savor completion until it registers. Look at the finished work. Let yourself enjoy the relief, pride, and possibilities it creates. Record the connection. “I approached something difficult, worked through it, and finished. I want another experience like that.”
  7. Feed the appetite through repetition. Choose another meaningful challenge tomorrow. Increase the demand as your capacity grows. Include recovery so you can return with attention and energy. Build a record of work you increasingly look forward to tackling.

Some sessions will feel unpleasant throughout. Record those honestly alongside the moments when effort becomes absorbing or satisfying. Let your growing appetite rest on experiences you can recognize and repeat.

I want the challenge.
Let me into the difficult part.
Find the satisfaction in the effort.
Enjoy gaining ground.
Finish it. Feel it. Come back hungry.
