Furious Action Plan + Friction Maxxing Plan

You become a person of ferocious action by getting good at the moment you usually switch away. You’ve already named the thought that makes the switch feel reasonable. “I should get organized first.” That’s the moment to practice.

Use CBT at the decision point
  1. Catch the thought. Say it in the exact words it came in. “I need a clear head before I can write this.”
  2. Turn it into a prediction. “If I start with a scattered head, I won’t produce anything I can use.”
  3. Run the experiment. Open the piece and draft the next scene for 20 minutes. Start with one thing you actually saw. At the end, look at what you wrote. Did you get a usable sentence? Did the scene come into focus as you worked?
  4. Return the instant you notice you’ve switched. Close the tab. Put your hands back on the work. Each time you return while the urge to leave is still there, you practice the behavior you want to be able to summon under pressure.
Give the hard work a finish line

Pick one thing to finish today that would change your situation. Define it in a sentence with a verb you can verify.

  • Writing: “Publish the essay.” If you’re still drafting, “Finish the draft through the last paragraph.”
  • Money: “Open the IRS notices, write down the tax years and balances, and make the call.”
  • Photography: “Make the edit, sequence the photographs, and export the set.”

Then do the part you’ve been working around. If you keep revising the opening, write the ending. If you keep organizing the tax folder, open the notice you’re avoiding. If you keep tweaking the edit, make the final cut.

Your cliff jump today is the first consequential action you’ve been postponing. Put the exact task in front of you, give it 20 minutes, and decide what comes next with evidence from the work itself. At the end of the day, write two lines. What did I finish? What thought did I act through?

I can feel the resistance and still move. Do the hard thing. Finish it.

Friction maxxing plan

Practice wanting the result strongly enough to accept the discomfort that comes with it. You can act before that discomfort becomes something you crave.

Make this a daily experiment.
  1. Make tomorrow’s reward concrete. Before starting, write one sentence. “Tomorrow morning, this listing will be live, buyers can find it, and I’ll have one fewer unfinished obligation.” Picture the actual consequence of finishing.
  2. Name today’s discomfort precisely. “I’m afraid of setting the price.” “This paragraph feels clumsy.” “I’m bored.” Then ask yourself, “Am I willing to feel this while I take the next step?”
  3. Choose productive friction. Approach the action that advances the outcome. Submit the application. Finish the ending. Make the call. Difficulty earns your time when it serves something you want.
  4. Stay through the first urge to escape. When you reach for another tab, pause and complete one more concrete step. Practice discovering what happens when you continue with the urge still present.
  5. Let completion register. When something is finished, stop for a moment. Look at the submitted application, published piece, or completed edit. Write down what it makes possible. Give yourself time to enjoy having followed through.
  6. Return tomorrow. Set a demanding workload you can repeat. Include sleep, meals, movement, and breaks in that commitment. Ambition needs your capacity across many days.

When the thought is “This hurts now, and the reward is too far away,” try a more credible response.

“Finishing gives me something I want. I can accept this much discomfort to move toward it.”

Measure the experiment by what you finish, how quickly you return after drifting, and whether you keep your commitments on ordinary days. You can build those behaviors even when the feeling of obsession never arrives.
