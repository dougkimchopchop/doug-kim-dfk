Structure

Structure is the order in which the reader finds things out. The material gives you what happened. Structure decides what it means, and when the reader gets it.

The rules · overall guidelines

My synthesis of the sources linked at the bottom. Attributed where one writer owns the idea.

1
Find the story inside the situation. What happened is not what the piece is about. The center sentence is how you find out.
“The situation is the context or circumstance, sometimes the plot; the story is the emotional experience that preoccupies the writer: the insight, the wisdom, the thing one has come to say.”Vivian Gornick, The Situation and the Story
2
Keep one question open. The reader should always want to know something: what happened, why you did it, what this has to do with that.
3
Order for the reader, not the calendar. Chronology is one choice among many. It’s often the right one, but choose it. Don’t default to it.
4
Every section changes something. New fact, new feeling, new understanding. If nothing changes, it’s description you’re fond of. Cut it or merge it.
5
Build a turn. Somewhere past the middle, what the reader thought this was about shifts. It can be one short sentence.
6
Run two selves, from the start. Phillip Lopate’s “double perspective”: the one who lived it and the one who understands it now. “The intelligent narrator must be present from page 1 onward!” Don’t save the thinking for the last paragraph.
7
Start late, leave early. Open as close to the question as you can stand. End when the change has landed, before you explain it.
8
Choose the shape after the dump. Your order stays dump → hunt → aim → build. Structure enters at “hunt.” Don’t pour raw experience into a shape you picked in advance, unless you’re doing a shape rep on purpose.
9
Make it invisible. John McPhee wants a shape that can’t be argued with, and that readers don’t notice. If they admire the braid, the braid is too loud.
How to structure a piece · Draft 2 instructions

Your Writing Plan says Draft 2 is “find the center.” This is that draft, step by step. Allow about 45 minutes before you rewrite anything.

1
Write the center sentence. “This is really about ___.” Use a person and a feeling. Write five if the first is a topic. Example of the difference: “Istanbul’s ferries” is a topic. It only becomes a center once it says what you wanted from them and didn’t get.
2
Reverse-outline. One line per paragraph, two parts: what it says and what it does (place, zoom, swerve, confess, undercut, land). Purdue’s version is for academic papers, but the method carries over.
3
Mark what changes. Next to each line, write what the reader knows or feels now that they didn’t before. Blank means cut or merge.
4
Find the turn. Circle where the piece shifts. No circle means no turn yet, and that’s the actual problem to solve. Look for a contradiction, an embarrassment or a detail you keep returning to.
5
Choose a shape. Use the table below. Your outline often already leans toward one.
6
Reorder on paper. Try three orders. Try opening on the turn. Try ending a paragraph earlier. Pick the order that keeps the question open longest without cheating.
7
Set the bookends. Delete the first paragraph (your Wednesday checklist already says so). Write three openings and three endings from the moves list. Check that the ending answers the opening, even if the answer is “I still don’t know.”
8
Now rewrite. From the outline, with the old draft closed. You’ll keep the good sentences, because you’ll remember them.
A worked map · “Shooting an Elephant”

This is what a finished reverse outline looks like, 14 paragraphs in 7 moves. Read the essay first if you can.

¶1–2 Confess
He’s hated, and he hates the job. Changes: we can’t trust him as a hero, so we watch him differently.
¶3–5 Incident
An elephant, conflicting reports, a dead man. Changes: the question becomes “will he have to shoot?”
¶6–7 Decide
A crowd follows. The animal is grazing peacefully, and he decides not to shoot. Changes: he’s named the right thing, so the fall has somewhere to fall from.
¶8 Turn
He sees about two thousand faces and knows he must. The essay’s idea arrives inside the scene, at its point of highest tension. Changes: the question flips, from “will he?” to “why can’t he not?”
¶9–10 Reason
He weighs the animal’s value and fears looking foolish. Changes: we watch him rationalize in real time.
¶11–13 Do it
A long, botched death, and he leaves before it’s over. Changes: the idea becomes a physical cost. No commentary.
¶14 Land
Everyone’s opinions, then the true motive: “solely to avoid looking a fool.” Changes: one plain sentence shrinks the whole empire down to one embarrassed man.
Steal this: confess early, decide against it, reverse in front of witnesses, put your big idea at the point of highest tension, and end on the smallest honest thing.

Nine shapes

Red chip marks the turn. Your Craft Catalog holds more author-by-author sequences. Models come from there and Essay Practice.

The widening encounter

Use it whenone meeting with a stranger holds more than the two of you. This is your book’s native shape.

Plain setupStranger + one detailYour small actOthers drawn inEarned statement

HowStart with logistics, not atmosphere. Bring the stranger in through one physical detail. Make your help or intrusion small and imperfect. Then let the circle grow. You may state the hope or the meaning outright at the end, because the scene has paid for it.

ReadNaomi Shihab Nye, “Gate A-4”, a prose poem: a four-hour delay, a crying woman, a few words of Arabic, phone calls, cookies, then “Not everything is lost.”

TryThe shepherd and the Korean ringtone, under 600 words. Stop writing the moment the frame has widened as far as it can go.

The public turn

Use it whenyou did something you can’t fully defend, and other people were watching.

Confess the positionIncidentDecide not toThe crowdDo it anywayConsequencesFlat admission

HowTell the reader your compromised position before anything happens. Set up the decent choice and have your narrator choose it. Then make the pressure visible, so the reader watches the reversal happen. End on the small true motive, not on a lesson.

ReadOrwell, “Shooting an Elephant”. Mapped paragraph by paragraph below.

TryA time the camera made you do something you wouldn’t have done without it. Write the decision to walk away first, then the reversal.

Present frame, long flashback

Use it whenone memory keeps returning and you still don’t understand it.

Now: “let me tell you”Summary of the timeOne daySceneSurprise“Years later”Now: open question

HowSpeak directly to the reader in the present. Give a short summary of the period, then narrow to “once.” Live through the scene moment by moment with plain language and clear statements of feeling. Let the older narrator interrupt once or twice. Come back to the present and admit what you still don’t know.

ReadRichard Hugo, “Ci Vediamo,” in your Writers Studio folder. It ends: “Do you understand? I’m not sure I do.” The course notes call out the absence of metaphor and the accumulation of events.

TryThe Anita piece. Keep the plain register, which was already its strongest, and use the refrain only where the older narrator interrupts.

Braid

Use it whenyou have a personal story that can’t carry the weight alone, or a subject too large to approach head on.

ABABAB meets AA+B

HowPick two or three strands that are clearly distinct in content or voice. Build them bit by bit, side by side, because each changes what the other needs. Use a visible break between strands. Don’t explain the link. The reader should be arriving at it just before you do.

ReadJo Ann Beard, “The Fourth State of Matter” (minor strands hide the major one). Biss, “Time and Distance Overcome” (telephone poles become racial violence). Teju Cole, “Black Body” (his Leukerbad trip woven with Baldwin’s essay).

TryIstanbul: a ferry crossing on one strand, one researched fact about the ferries on the other. Six fragments, three each, no transitions.

Segments

Use it whenthe material is broken, or you can only reach it from the side.

FragmentgapFragmentgapShort linegapFragmentReturn

HowWrite sections that could almost stand alone. Vary their length, and let some be one line. Order them for the charge between neighbors, then move them around until a new meaning shows up. Numbers or titles are optional. Montserrat Carty’s test: can the reader leap each gap and feel the connection? Gaps nobody can cross are a way of avoiding the work.

ReadAbigail Thomas, A Three Dog Life. The catastrophe is told flat and early, then the book moves in small domestic sections.

TryFive photographs from one trip, one section each, 100 words. Then shuffle the order three times and keep the one that surprises you.

The walk and the return

Use it whenthe piece is a wander, and a wander needs a spine. Closest to “Wander” itself.

Trivial errandOutStrangerStrangerSelf dissolvesHomeSame object

HowGive the walk a small, almost silly excuse. Alternate general claims with specific sights, and support each claim with a scene. Come home to where you started. The object is unchanged, and the reader isn’t.

ReadVirginia Woolf, “Street Haunting”. She goes out for a lead pencil, loses herself in other lives, and ends back in her room holding the pencil. The online copy needs a sign-in, and it’s also in your Writers Studio folder.

TryYour excuse for leaving the house with a camera on one specific day. Close on the thing you came home with.

The zoom

Use it whenone small fact can grow until it’s about everything.

Tiny factBiggerBiggerUsOne long last sentence

HowOpen by asking the reader to look at something small. Keep every step concrete and sized up from the last. Don’t enter as “I” until you have to. Save your longest sentence for the end. You can also run it the other way, from a physical fact to a feeling never named.

ReadBrian Doyle, “Joyas Voladoras” goes from a hummingbird’s heart to a whale’s to ours. Solnit, “The Blue of Distance” goes from the physics of light to longing.

TryOne technical fact about film, or about a clock mechanism for the man with the clocks. Five steps up in scale, five sentences each.

The borrowed form

Use it whenplain telling turns sentimental, and a cold container would hold it better.

Official formOfficial formVoice leaksForm breaksLast line

HowUse a real form and its real headings. Keep its language straight for as long as you can stand to. The Pendarvis exercise from your course gives the engine: the official voice loses control, and the need to speak wins out. Escalate the stakes as you go.

ReadRandon Billings Noble, “The Heart as a Torn Muscle” (patient handout). Brenda Miller, “We Regret to Inform You” (rejection letters). Marjie Alonso, “Lebanese Eggs” (recipe).

TryA photo release form, a caption sheet or a customs declaration. Fill it in for the birthday party you crashed in Bucharest.

Four acts, late twist

Use it whenthere’s no conflict to resolve, only something you didn’t see coming. Good for gentle travel material that feels like it has no plot.

IntroduceDevelopNew elementEverything rhymes

HowHenry Lien’s retrofit: name three main elements. Hold one back until the third act. Make the first two acts engaging without it, which he says is the hardest part. In the fourth act, show the relationships nobody could see before.

ReadKnown as kishōtenketsu in Japanese and gi seung jeon gyeol in Korean. Christine Hyung-Oak Lee’s memoir-specific take is partly paywalled.

TryTake a Dispatch that feels flat. Find what you left out because it seemed unrelated, and bring it in at the 60% mark.

Moves · openings, turns, endings
Open on
A stark fact. Baldwin dates the day and states it.
A strange image, unexplained. Dillard’s clown painting.
A claim you’ll undo. Didion’s “we,” which erodes into “I.”
An offer to the reader. Hugo: “I’ll tell you some stories.” Doyle: “Consider the hummingbird.”
Plain logistics. Nye’s four-hour delay.
Turn with
A short question. After a long lyric paragraph on the desert, Solnit asks “And the man?”
A plain personal sentence after pages of facts. Biss.
A witness. Orwell’s crowd.
A swerve in attention. Hugo sits down to rest by a field of grass, and the piece changes consciousness.
A new element. Lien’s asteroid.
End on
The small true motive. Orwell.
The opening object, changed. Woolf’s pencil.
An open question to the reader. Hugo.
A short line that turns the whole thing. White’s “chill of death.”
An earned plain statement. Nye.
An image that won’t explain itself. Biss.
Fixes · symptom → move
SymptomFix
Reads like “and then, and then”Replace some “and then” with “but” or “so.” If you can’t, those events aren’t connected. Cut or braid them.
Every part is beautiful, but nothing’s happeningSituation, no story. Redo the center sentence. Who’s at stake?
Starts three paragraphs too earlyOpen on the first sentence a stranger would need to read on.
All the meaning is in the last paragraphMove one piece of thinking to page one, and one into the scene at its most tense.
Several endings in a rowFind the first one that lands. Delete the rest.
Drifts into manifestoGo back to a specific person doing a specific thing. Let one general claim survive.
Fragments feel randomAdd a refrain, a returning image or a repeating form. Check the gaps can be leapt.
Sags in the middleThe question closed too early. Hold back one fact.
